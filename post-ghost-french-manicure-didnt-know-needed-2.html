The Ghost-French Manicure You Didn't Know You Needed

Sept 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Psychologist Robert Zajonc's research on the mere exposure effect found something almost counterintuitive: people tend to develop a stronger preference for something simply through repeated exposure to it, independent of any new information about why it's good. The ghost-French manicure — a classic French shape with a small ghost swapped in at the tip — follows exactly that pattern: it looks like a minor novelty the first time, and steadily more appealing every time it resurfaces after that.

Why it grows on you instead of hitting all at once

The first glance at a ghost-French might not register as anything special — it's a subtle variation on a design you already know well. Per the mere exposure effect, that familiarity is exactly what sets up the growing appeal: each additional time you see the design, the preference compounds a little, until it reads as an obvious favorite rather than a novelty.

Familiar shape plus one repeated new element

This is part of why the design works so well as a slow-burn favorite: the French shape underneath is already familiar, so the mere-exposure effect only has to work on the one new element — the small ghost — rather than an entirely unfamiliar design.

You don't have to love something the first time you see it. Sometimes liking it is just a matter of seeing it again.

The fresh reframe

If the ghost-French manicure didn't grab you immediately, that's not a verdict. Per the research on how preference actually forms, it might just need one more look.
